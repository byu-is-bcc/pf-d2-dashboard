## What this project is

Take a trained model and stand it up as a service a stranger can call from a browser. Then build one small thing that consumes it over the network, deployed separately, so the two have to talk over HTTP.

A model that only exists in a notebook is not a product. This project is the step where it becomes one, and the hard part is not FastAPI. The hard part is that the thing you deployed has to agree with the thing you trained, and there is no error message when it does not.

**Your claim, the first line of your finished README:**

> "Serves predictions at `<N>` ms median latency, deployed at `<URL>`, called by `<the thing you built>`."

Three concrete facts, none of which you can fake. Verify it against [the checklist](#how-to-verify-this) before you publish, because that is the list a reviewer uses.

**Builds naturally on [D1 — One Number](https://github.com/byu-is-bcc/pf-d1-one-number).** If you did D1, bring your model here and skip the toy one. If you did not, the starter model is trained and ready, and you can do this project standalone.

## This repository ships with a bug in it

**The most common real bug in student ML deployment is that training and serving preprocess data differently.** You fit a scaler on your training data, you save the model, and then the service applies scaling of its own. The two do not match. Predictions still come back. Nothing errors. They are just quietly wrong, and every downstream number you publish is wrong with them.

**This repository has that bug right now.** If you wire up `/predict` and deploy it as it stands, you will have a live service returning plausible, confident, incorrect probabilities. Nothing will tell you.

Your job, in order:

**1. Find where the two paths disagree.** Do not read for it, measure for it. Take a handful of rows out of `data/support_tickets.csv`, score them the way `train.py` scores its held-out set, then score the same rows through the service, and compare the numbers row by row. Two paths running the same model over the same inputs have no legitimate reason to differ in the third decimal place, let alone the first. When you find the gap, work backward from it until you can say exactly which values differ and why.

**2. Fix it by serializing one object.** Build a `sklearn.pipeline.Pipeline` containing both the preprocessing and the estimator, fit that, and `joblib.dump` the pipeline. Then the serving code calls `.predict_proba()` on the pipeline and there is no second place for preprocessing to live, which means there is no second place for it to drift. The fix is smaller than the bug: it usually deletes more code than it adds.

**3. Write the test that would have caught it.** `tests/test_contract.py` has a stub named `test_training_and_serving_agree_on_the_same_rows`. That is the one. It runs on every retrain forever, and it is worth more than the fix, because the fix protects you today and the test protects you in March.

**Then write it up in `MODEL_CARD.md`,** under known limitations: what the disagreement was, what it did to the numbers, and what now prevents it. Finding your own bug and documenting it is a hiring signal. Quietly fixing it is not.

If you swap in your own model from D1, do not assume you left the bug behind. Rebuild it as a pipeline from the start.

## Your job

**1. Wire up `/predict`.** The request and response contracts are defined and the TODOs in `app/main.py` list the steps. Decide what a request that validates but is nonsense should get, and decide your threshold.

**2. Fix the training-serving disagreement.** The section above. This is the project.

**3. Write the contract tests.** Three stubs, one asserting the success shape, one asserting malformed input is refused, one asserting the two paths agree. `tests/test_smoke.py` is the bar to match.

**4. Finish `scripts/measure_latency.py`** and measure at least 100 requests against your deployed service. Not localhost. Localhost latency is a statement about your laptop.

**5. Fill in `MODEL_CARD.md`.** Four sections, twenty minutes, and it is the part of your repository that reads as professional rather than academic.

**6. Deploy the service.** Below.

**7. Build the consumer and deploy it separately.** A page, a script, a spreadsheet add-in. `consumer/index.html` is a starting point. The requirement is the network boundary: your model and its caller must be two deployed things talking over HTTP, because that is what the boundary looks like at work. Host the page on your Project 000 site and you have satisfied two requirements at once.

**8. Add it to your Project 000 site.**

## Deploying

Both platforms have a free tier, neither needs Docker, and both watch your repository and redeploy on push.

**Railway.** New project, deploy from GitHub repo, pick this one. `railway.json` supplies the build and start commands and points the healthcheck at `/health`. Generate a public domain under Settings, Networking.

**Render.** New Web Service, connect the repository. `render.yaml` is a blueprint, so you can instead use New, Blueprint and Render reads the config from the file.

Both configs run `python train.py` during the build, because the artifact is gitignored. Read the comment in `render.yaml` about why that is a tradeoff rather than a best practice, and be ready to say what you would do instead at a company.

Three things that will happen to you:

- **The free tier sleeps.** The first request after idle takes several seconds. Say so in your README and exclude warmup requests from your latency measurement. `measure_latency.py` already has a `--warmup` flag.
- **`$PORT` is injected.** Bind to it. Hardcoding 8000 is the single most common reason a deploy builds fine and then never answers.
- **CORS will block your consumer.** Your page is on one origin and your API is on another, and the browser cares even though `curl` does not. That is the network boundary being real. Add `CORSMiddleware` and decide which origins you allow rather than reflexively allowing all of them.

Secrets go in the platform's environment variable settings, never in a committed file. `.env.example` documents what exists; `.env` is gitignored.

## The failing tests are intentional

CI runs two jobs and they are supposed to disagree.

**`smoke (must stay green)`** proves the app imports, the model trains and loads, and `/health` answers over a real HTTP connection. If this goes red you broke the scaffolding, not the assignment.

**`contract tests (red until you write them)`** is red the moment you click "Use this template", because those tests are stubs. That red X is the assignment, visible from your repository page, and it turns green when you finish. Leaving it red is how a reviewer knows you did not.

Run `pytest -v` locally and you will see the same split: smoke passing, contract failing with a message telling you what to write.

## How to verify this

Same list an alumni reviewer will use. Do it yourself first.

- [ ] Public `/docs` page a stranger can use without credentials or setup
- [ ] `/health` endpoint returning service and model status
- [ ] Median and p95 latency measured over at least 100 requests, with the measurement script committed
- [ ] At least one contract test asserting the response shape, and one asserting behavior on malformed input
- [ ] The preprocessing pipeline is serialized **with** the model, so the service cannot silently disagree with training
- [ ] A model card covering training data, class balance, intended use, and known limitations
- [ ] Secrets in environment variables, `.env.example` committed, `.env` gitignored
- [ ] The consumer is a separately deployed thing calling your service over HTTP
- [ ] No secrets in git history: `git log --all --diff-filter=A --name-only | grep -i env`

**That fifth item is the one that matters.** If you can only verify one thing, verify that, because it is the only item on the list that fails without telling you. A missing `/health` endpoint is obvious. A service that scales its inputs differently than training did looks completely fine from the outside and is wrong on every request.

Here is how you verify it, concretely: score the same rows through the training path and through the deployed service and require the probabilities to match to a tight tolerance. If reproducing the training path is awkward, that is the finding, not an obstacle. It means your artifact does not contain everything your predictions depend on.

**Deploy before you call it done.** A model on localhost is not an artifact a recruiter can open in 30 seconds.

## Where people get stuck

**"It works locally."** Then it works on your laptop, with your Python version, your installed packages, and your file paths. Deploy it. Roughly half of the problems in this project only exist on the other side of that step.

**"My predictions look reasonable."** Reasonable is not a test. The bug this project is about produces reasonable-looking output on every request. Score the same rows both ways and compare the numbers.

**"I cannot reproduce the training path from the artifact."** Correct, and that is the whole lesson. Fix the artifact rather than working around it.

**"How do I know my latency number is real?"** Measure the deployed service, over at least 100 requests, discarding warmup. Report the median and the p95 together. A median alone hides exactly the requests users complain about, and a p95 from 20 samples is one unlucky network hiccup.

**"CORS."** Yes. Your consumer is on a different origin than your API and the browser is enforcing that. Add `CORSMiddleware` in `app/main.py` and list the origins you actually want. Reaching for `allow_origins=["*"]` works and is worth being able to explain rather than just doing.

**"Do I need a database?"** No. The brief allows SQLite or Supabase if you log predictions, and logging predictions is a nice addition, but a service with no persistence satisfies every requirement on the checklist.

**"Can I use something other than FastAPI?"** Yes. The reference is FastAPI because its automatic `/docs` page directly serves the "a recruiter can POST to your model in 30 seconds" requirement. If you use Flask or ASP.NET Core, add an equivalent interactive docs page. Every requirement on the checklist is framework-independent, including the one about the pipeline.
